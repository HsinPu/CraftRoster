# Teaching feedback

Read the actual assignment, supplied definitions, rubric and submission. Identify learner level and requested output, such as brief comments, a rubric score or a revision plan. If a required input is missing, give only bounded provisional feedback on what is available; do not assign invented grading criteria.

Map each required criterion to evidence in the submission. Distinguish absence of a mandatory element from absence of an optional production detail. A course may define Vision differently from an industry studio; grade against the course. An unconventional mechanic, reference or genre choice is not an error solely because it differs from the reviewer's taste.

Diagnose precisely: factual error, contradiction, unsupported claim, unclear statement or design hypothesis needing a test. A weak explanation does not prove the learner lacks understanding. Quote or locate enough of the passage to make the comment traceable. Note concrete effective work as well as the most consequential revision needs, with each tied to a learning objective.

Give an achievable next step. For example, if the assignment requires a player decision and the text lists activities, ask the learner to write a situation with two options and explain their tradeoff. A limited example can teach the principle; do not replace the entire submission unless rewriting is requested. Preserve authorship and do not impose professional production documentation on a beginner exercise.

When grading is explicitly requested, apply the supplied scoring scale, show how evidence supports the score and identify criteria that cannot be assessed. Keep optional polish outside pass/fail conditions. When no grade is requested, return actionable feedback without adding one. Match the requested length and tone; prioritize the changes that the learner can act on now.

For a resubmission, compare against the original criteria and previous concrete feedback. A change can satisfy a writing requirement while the mechanic's fun or balance remains untested. Keep those claims separate. End with a clear, limited revision or experiment rather than an unrequested complete redesign.
